03 / Clinical — Behind the chair

First-year rotations:
watching closely, asking questions, learning how a clinic breathes.

“During these rotations I had the opportunity to observe and participate in various learning activities across four departments. The exposure helped me connect theoretical concepts with real clinical situations — and improved my communication, observation, teamwork, and professional conduct.”

1

Community Dentistry

Oral health begins outside the clinic — in schools, homes and everyday habits. On a school outreach session I assisted with screening children for visible caries, demonstrated brushing technique on a model, and handed out oral hygiene kits under the eye of a senior public-health officer. It widened the frame: prevention at scale needs a clear message, a repeatable protocol, and trust built in the first thirty seconds.

2

Communication Skills

A clinician’s first instrument is a conversation. We role-played patient interviews in pairs — delivering a multi-visit treatment plan to an anxious “patient” — and practised the SPIKES framework for breaking difficult news, with peer feedback on tone, pacing and non-verbal cues. I learned to open a conversation, hold one, and close one with care; and to let silence do some of the work.

3

Radiology

Learning to read the stories teeth tell in shadow and light. I observed intraoral periapical (IOPA) views, a panoramic radiograph (OPG) and a cone-beam CT for implant planning — watching how positioning, exposure and patient cooperation make or break image quality. By the end of the week my pattern recognition had grown enough to point out obvious caries and trace the lamina dura around a few teeth; what is there, what is missing, what is beginning.

4

Oral Medicine & Diagnostics

Where careful questions become careful answers. I observed history-taking and examination of patients presenting with oral ulcers, white patches and suspected premalignant lesions, and was walked through a head-and-neck examination sequence and a structured history template — onset, duration, aggravating factors, systemic context. The discipline of looking before touching, and of building a differential that doesn’t anchor on the first plausible answer.

The reflections — in my own words

Reflection 01

Community Dentistry

01Description
I joined a community dentistry outreach session organized for a local school. My role was to assist with screening children for visible caries, demonstrate brushing technique on a model, and hand out oral hygiene kits. The session was supervised by a senior dental public health officer, who guided us through the patient flow and the recording of basic findings on the field form.
02Feelings
I was initially unsure how to keep a row of waiting children engaged, and I worried that my screening notes might be inconsistent. As the morning went on, I grew more confident because the workflow became predictable and the supervisor gave brief, encouraging feedback after each group.
03Evaluation
The positive side was real-world interaction: I saw how social context shapes oral health behaviour, not just clinical findings. The negative side was that I struggled to keep my screening time consistent — I spent longer with cooperative children and rushed through the more reluctant ones.
04Analysis
Community dentistry complements chair-side work by treating populations instead of individual patients. I realised that prevention at scale depends on three things: a clear message, a short and repeatable screening protocol, and trust built in the first thirty seconds of contact. My time management issue reflected a protocol that I had internalised rather than written down.
05Conclusion
If I had a short, printed screening checklist in hand, I could keep the pace consistent without sacrificing rapport. More importantly, I understood that community work is not a softer form of dentistry — it requires deliberate preparation, because there is no unit-chair to fall back on.
06Action plan
I will prepare a one-page screening checklist before the next outreach, time each child for a maximum of two minutes, and volunteer for the post-session briefing so I can present findings and receive structured feedback from the supervisor.

Reflection 02

Communication Skills

01Description
In the communication skills rotation we role-played patient interviews in pairs, with one student playing a patient who had just been told they needed a multi-visit treatment plan. We practiced a structured framework (SPIKES) for breaking news, took simulated case histories, and received peer feedback on tone, pacing, and non-verbal cues.
02Feelings
I was nervous before the first role-play because I worried about sounding rehearsed or cold. Once I let the patient speak without interrupting, I felt calmer and more present, and I noticed that the silence after a piece of bad news was the hardest part to tolerate.
03Evaluation
What worked well was active listening — leaning forward, mirroring the patient’s posture, and summarising what they said before responding. What did not work was my tendency to fill silences too quickly, which undercut the patient’s chance to process the information.
04Analysis
Communication in dentistry is not soft skill padding; it is the bridge between diagnosis and consent. A patient who feels heard will share symptoms more accurately, accept the proposed plan, and return for follow-up. The framework helped because it gave me permission to pause — the silence is part of the treatment, not a gap in it.
05Conclusion
I should slow down. The next time I deliver news I will allow at least five seconds of silence after the key sentence, and check understanding by asking the patient to restate the plan in their own words rather than asking only if they have questions.
06Action plan
I will record one role-play per week and review it against a simple checklist: did I introduce myself, listen first, allow silence, summarise, and check understanding? I will also ask a senior to observe a real case and give written feedback.

Reflection 03

Radiology

01Description
During the radiology rotation I observed the acquisition of intraoral periapical (IOPA) views, a panoramic radiograph (OPG), and one cone-beam CT (CBCT) for implant planning. I learned how positioning, exposure parameters, and patient cooperation affect image quality, and I followed along as the supervisor interpreted each image.
02Feelings
The physics of beam angle and exposure felt overwhelming at first, and I was frustrated that I could not yet read anatomical landmarks at the same speed as the supervisor. By the end of the week my pattern recognition had improved enough that I could point out the obvious caries and the lamina dura around a few teeth.
03Evaluation
I performed well on positioning for IOPA views and on identifying anatomical landmarks such as the mental foramen and the maxillary sinus floor. I was weaker at picking up early inter-proximal caries and at distinguishing early peri-apical pathosis from normal variations.
04Analysis
A radiograph is an adjunct, not a verdict — it must always be correlated with the patient’s history and clinical findings, because a shadow on the image is meaningless without a symptom to attach it to. Reading images quickly is a product of seeing many images, not a product of natural ability, so the path forward is volume and feedback.
05Conclusion
If I had access to a structured atlas and a daily habit of looking at a handful of normal variants, I would stop second-guessing every lamina dura and start reserving attention for genuinely suspicious findings. My weakness is therefore a study habit gap, not a knowledge ceiling.
06Action plan
I will spend fifteen minutes a day with a recognised oral radiology atlas, present one interesting case per week in the journal club, and ask the supervisor to point out three normal variants on every shift so I can build a baseline before focusing on pathology.

Reflection 04

Oral Medicine & Diagnostics

01Description
In the oral medicine and diagnostics rotation I observed history-taking and examination of patients presenting with oral ulcers, white patches, and suspected premalignant lesions. The senior registrar walked me through a head-and-neck examination sequence and a structured history template that captures onset, duration, aggravating factors, and systemic context.
02Feelings
Some of the patient stories were emotionally heavy, especially when a lesion had been ignored for months. I had to consciously maintain composure during the examination, and I felt the gap between my textbook list of differentials and the registrar’s short, focused one.
03Evaluation
My systematic approach to examination was a strength — I did not skip steps, and the registrar commented that the order of the steps was correct. My weakness was in generating a complete differential; I would anchor on the first plausible diagnosis and forget to include the rarer but more serious possibilities.
04Analysis
A methodical head-to-toe examination catches what a quick look misses, but a methodical examination is only useful if paired with an equally methodical differential. The registrar’s short list was not the result of skipping, but of years of pattern weighting; my long, disorganised list was a sign of inexperience, not of being thorough.
05Conclusion
I need a written differential checklist by symptom — for example, every white patch should trigger a fixed sequence (leukoplakia, lichen planus, candidiasis, frictional keratosis, malignancy) rather than a free recall. Otherwise I will keep anchoring on whichever diagnosis I read most recently.
06Action plan
I will maintain a small clinical diary of cases seen, with one differential checklist per symptom category. I will also ask the supervisor to mark the cases where my differential missed an important possibility, so I can review those symptom categories at the end of each week.
